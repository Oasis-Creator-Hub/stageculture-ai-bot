Plate XCI · On things that cannot be repeated

Sensitive Dependence

Seven double pendulums, one starting position, and a disagreement about the fourth decimal place.

A single pendulum is the most obedient object in physics. Hang a second one from its tip and obedience ends. Nothing has been added but a hinge, and no law has been broken, yet the motion that follows cannot be forecast for more than a few breaths.

The seven pendulums on the left are not copies in the loose sense. They are the same pendulum, lifted to the same height, except that each begins one ten-thousandth of a radian further round than its neighbour. That is less than the width of a hair seen from across a room. While they agree, their coloured trails add up to a single white line.

The rule is perfectly known. Only the present is slightly out of focus, and that turns out to be enough.

Watch the white fray. First a fringe of red and violet at the outside of the fastest turns, then whole ribbons, then seven instruments playing seven tunes. The second figure shows why it feels sudden: the gap was growing all along, by a steady factor each second, and it only became visible when it reached the size of things you can see.

This is what the word chaos means in a laboratory. It is not disorder. Each arm obeys Newton to the last digit. It means that small ignorance compounds like interest, and that any forecast has a horizon beyond which better instruments buy only a little more time.

    Integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta at 480 steps per second; equal arms, equal masses, no friction. Start from a low hang and the seven will stay together far longer. Chaos here needs height.
